# Editorial Guide

This document defines the default editing language for videos produced with this pipeline. It is a decision framework, not a mandate to add more effects. The goal is clear storytelling, convincing motion, strong audiovisual impact, and proof that survives scrutiny.

## 1. Governing priority

Choose cuts in this order:

1. Action and motion timing.
2. Story clarity and viewer comprehension.
3. Sound-effect timing.
4. Musical structure and beats.
5. Transition style.

Do not cut only because a beat exists. If the action reads better a few frames before or after the beat, preserve the action.

A useful default for important moments is:

**setup -> impact -> recovery**

The viewer needs enough setup to understand what is about to happen, a decisive impact or reveal, and enough recovery time to register the result.

A related rhythm is:

**anticipation -> release -> result**

A brief hold before the release often gives the following hit, reveal, machine movement, explosion, or transition more weight.

## 2. Cut design

### Prefer hard cuts

Hard cuts are the default. Dissolves, zoom transitions, spins, wipes, and other stylized transitions need a visual or narrative reason.

Do not use a decorative transition to hide weak shot selection.

### Hide cuts inside motion

When continuity benefits from an invisible-feeling cut, place it inside strong visual motion:

- camera whip or rapid pan;
- character movement;
- foreground occlusion;
- an object crossing frame;
- smoke, sparks, flashes, explosions, or particles;
- a fast mechanical movement;
- another visually dominant event.

Match motion direction where possible so the next shot inherits momentum.

### Do not cut on every beat

Mechanical beat cutting makes the edit predictable. Let some shots begin before a beat, land just after it, or span several beats. The image should feel musical without behaving like a metronome.

### Vary cut density

Use faster cutting for escalation, pursuit, assembly, rapid comparison, or compressed progress. Use longer shots for suspense, scale, proof, emotional weight, or a payoff.

Fast only works when the edit also knows how to slow down.

## 3. Shot progression and visual information

Avoid long runs of shots with the same scale and purpose. Useful progressions include:

- wide -> medium -> close -> detail;
- detail -> detail -> reveal;
- establishing wide -> POV/inspection -> tracking shot -> extreme wide payoff;
- before -> operation -> after.

Each cut should add information, change perspective, increase stakes, or improve rhythm.

For demonstrations, establish geography early enough that the viewer understands where the action occurs. When scale is the point, include at least one shot that makes the scale unambiguous.

## 4. Proof shots

When a video is demonstrating a technical claim, do not cut away from the exact moment that proves it.

Examples include:

- crossing a world-height or area boundary;
- a machine completing an uninterrupted cycle;
- a benchmark counter changing;
- a before/after state transition;
- continuous movement through a supposedly impossible space;
- a system recovering from an event.

Prefer a longer, mostly uninterrupted proof shot over an aggressively edited montage. Coordinates, height, counters, landmarks, or other persistent reference cues can strengthen the proof.

Do not sacrifice credibility for pace.

## 5. Music

Music should have structure rather than permanent intensity. A common shape is:

**intro -> build -> breakdown or restraint -> major event/payoff -> final release**

Reserve the strongest musical section for the strongest visual section.

Avoid stacking the loudest music under every important event. If everything is at maximum intensity, nothing feels important.

### Strategic silence

Silence or near-silence is a deliberate tool. A short drop in music or ambience immediately before a reveal, hit, failure, transformation, or large-scale event can make the following sound and image much stronger.

Do not fear empty space.

## 6. Sound design

Treat sound as a layered system rather than one oversized impact sample.

Use clean cuts and natural source audio by default. Synthetic transition whooshes
and risers are opt-in only and require an explicit request. Available SFX tooling
does not make those effects a required production step.

A strong event may combine:

- environmental bed or room/game ambience;
- natural movement sounds;
- mechanical or physical impact;
- low-frequency weight;
- debris, crackle, resonance, or tail;
- reverb appropriate to the space.

Leave room between major impacts. Continuous maximum loudness removes contrast.

Preserve real environmental texture: machinery, footsteps, wind, UI/mechanical cues, engines, tools, water, or other source audio that makes the footage feel physically grounded.

### Audio across cuts

Use audio continuity to bind shots:

- let ambience continue across a visual cut;
- allow a sound to begin before the new shot when it helps anticipation;
- let impact/reverb continue after the image changes;
- use J-cuts and L-cuts when they improve continuity or narrative flow.

The audio edit does not need to share the exact boundary of the video edit.

### Sonic motifs

A recurring sound or small sound family can identify a character, machine, mechanic, location, or recurring concept. Repetition should create recognition, not noise.

## 7. Speed changes

Use speed ramps only around motivated movement.

Good uses include accelerating into an action, compressing dead travel, or easing into the exact frame that matters. Random ramps used as decoration quickly cheapen the edit.

Never speed through the moment that the viewer needs to inspect as proof.

## 8. Narration

Narration should add context, stakes, reasoning, or meaning. It should not merely describe what the viewer can already see.

Weak:
“The elevator is going up.”

Stronger:
“This crossing is normally outside the playable vertical range.”

Keep narration sparse enough that source sound, music, and visuals can carry part of the story.

For showcase videos, prefer short declarative lines over dense explanation during the most visually important moments.

## 9. On-screen text

Text should communicate evidence or orientation, not fill empty space.

Useful overlays include:

- coordinates or height;
- benchmark values;
- feature names;
- short before/after labels;
- constraints or test conditions;
- concise chapter/location identifiers.

Keep overlays brief, readable, and on screen long enough to be understood. Avoid decorative text that competes with the footage.

## 10. Opening hook

For YouTube-style showcase videos, the first roughly 0-15 seconds should establish the strongest reason to keep watching.

Prefer:

1. an impossible-looking result, striking failure, transformation, or high-value outcome;
2. just enough context to make the viewer ask how it happened;
3. then the setup.

Avoid long logos, greetings, menu navigation, or background explanation before the first meaningful visual.

## 11. Technical showcase pattern

A strong short technical showcase often follows this shape:

1. **Hook** — show the extraordinary result immediately.
2. **Context** — establish the normal constraint or problem.
3. **Setup** — show the system, build, or mechanism that will solve it.
4. **Progression** — accelerate through intermediate steps with tighter cuts.
5. **Proof** — slow down and hold the decisive continuous shot.
6. **Payoff** — use the strongest musical and visual moment after proof is clear.
7. **Hero/end shot** — finish with a clean view of the completed result.

For vertical traversal, world-boundary, transport, factory, or infrastructure showcases, keep persistent landmarks or coordinates when useful and let the critical crossing play substantially uninterrupted.

## 12. Editorial review checklist

Before rendering a final timeline, verify:

- Every cut has an action, story, sound, or rhythm reason.
- Important actions have readable setup and recovery.
- Cut density changes over the video instead of staying uniformly fast.
- Shot scales and purposes vary.
- Decorative transitions have been removed unless justified.
- Proof moments are long and clear enough to believe.
- Music has a deliberate intensity curve.
- At least one major moment uses contrast, restraint, or silence when appropriate.
- SFX are layered without masking source ambience.
- Audio is allowed to cross visual edit points when useful.
- Speed ramps are motivated by motion.
- Narration adds information instead of describing the picture.
- Text overlays provide evidence or orientation.
- The opening delivers a meaningful hook before extended explanation.
- The strongest visual and strongest musical section are not wasted early unless the hook specifically requires it.

When two rules conflict, preserve comprehension and credibility first. Style comes after the viewer understands and believes what happened.
